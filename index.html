Closed-loop integration

AI analyses user behaviour, performance or physiological signals and updates the immersive task or feedback in real time.

Common pattern in rehabilitation: adaptive feedback and assistance during training.

12 studies

One-way integration

AI generates information such as segmentation, registration or planning outputs, which XR presents within an immersive or spatial interface.

Common pattern in surgery: visualising anatomy for planning and navigation.

11 studies

Data-driven offline integration

XR elicits or structures data that AI analyses after or outside the immersive session, without real-time feedback into the environment.

Common pattern in mental health: analysing behavioural, speech or physiological data.

About the review

A systematic view of clinically relevant AI–XR research

We synthesised peer-reviewed empirical research in which AI and XR were functionally connected within the same clinical task, system, workflow or data pathway. Studies involved human participants or human participant data.

Read the abstract

The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Extended Reality (XR) offers opportunities to develop more adaptive, interactive, and context-aware healthcare systems. However, evidence on how these technologies are integrated into clinically relevant applications remains fragmented, with substantial variation in their roles, technologies, users, and evaluation approaches. This systematic review synthesises 41 clinically relevant empirical studies to characterise AI-XR integration across application domains, users, AI methods, XR technologies, integration approaches, reported outcomes, and clinical implementation maturity. Following PRISMA guidelines, the review systematically identified and analysed how existing studies combine AI and XR across medical applications. The findings show that AI-XR research has progressed beyond proof-of-principle, with systems increasingly evaluated with patients, clinicians, and clinical data across diverse applications. AI-XR applications were concentrated in surgery, mental health, and rehabilitation. Distinct integration modes emerged, with the relationship between AI and XR shaped primarily by the clinical task rather than the AI method or XR hardware. Despite generally positive reported outcomes, evidence remained concentrated in small-scale feasibility and pilot studies, with no study demonstrating large-scale testing or routine clinical adoption. These findings provide a structured view of the emerging AI-XR landscape and highlight the importance of task-specific integration, clinically meaningful interaction, and stronger evidence of implementation in real-world settings.

The five research questions
  1. What clinical domains and users are represented in AI-XR applications?
  2. What AI methods and XR technologies are used?
  3. How are AI and XR integrated within clinical tasks?
  4. What outcomes have been reported, and how mature is clinical implementation?
  5. What trends, limitations, and evidence gaps characterise AI-XR integration in healthcare?
